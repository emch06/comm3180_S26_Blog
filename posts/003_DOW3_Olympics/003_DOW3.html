What makes up an ideal hockey player?

Since women’s hockey officially became an Olympic sport in 1998, either USA or Canada has won each winter olympics cycle.

Olympic Women’s Hockey Gold Medal Winners

  • 2022 (Beijing): Canada
  • 2018 (Pyeongchang): USA
  • 2014 (Sochi): Canada
  • 2010 (Vancouver): Canada
  • 2006 (Torino): Canada
  • 2002 (Salt Lake City): Canada
  • 1998 (Nagano): USA

This made wonder what makes these two teams so dominant in ice hockey? And are there traits that can make up an ideal hockey player?

Dominance and winning cannot generally be attributed to physique alone. Canada has historically dominated the gold medal pool, but it is not like Canadian players are significantly bigger in size than the U.S.. Physical profiles are nearly identical, and sometimes the U.S. captures Gold. So the explanation likely falls elsewhere.

Based on the regional concentrations, it seemed like most of the players on the rosters were primarily from hockey-heavy regions which might be characteristic that defines an ideal hockey player.

From what I gathered from the women’s successful hockey players…

The ideal women’s hockey player should be…

  • around 169-171 cm tall
  • 67-70 kg
  • From a hockey-heavy region

Obviously, it would be difficult to compare height and weight of men’s hockey player to a women’s. But one consistent attribute I see all around between both the men and women seem to be that most winning hockey players are born in hockey-heavy regions where hockey is the culture and main sport.

CONCLUSION:

  • The data does not point to a definitive answer of an ideal hockey player. However, it does reveal a consistent pattern: a significant number of Olympic athletes were born in hockey-heavy regions. For Canada, it was Ontario and Quebec. While in the United States, Massachusetts and Minnesota. And for Russia, Moskva. This might suggest that although a competitive athlete has to fit a certain physical frame, winning Gold may be less dependent on individual physical variations and more about having accessibility to strong developmental programs, competitive youth leagues, and just the culture in general growing up. Maybe accessibility and the system set as a youth hockey player is the most important? A next step in this analysis would be to examine the average age of Olympic players at the time of competition.
